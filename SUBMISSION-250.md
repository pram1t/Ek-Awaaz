India's grievance portal asks you to name the office that owns your problem: 92 central organisations, then a category, then a sub-category. Ek Awaaz never asks.

Say what happened — in your words, or your voice. Smiti Didi asks two or three questions and answers aloud in the language you spoke, detected from the audio, not picked from a menu. Eleven Indian languages, heard and spoken. Built for the person the current portal was not.

**Route across five constitutional tiers** — Panchayat, municipal, state, central scheme, Union — naming the officer and the provision that makes them answerable. No central ministry can fix a village road.

**Name the stronger remedy you already hold, before you file.** Twelve mapped, eleven live: the RBI Ombudsman can award ₹20 lakh; NFSA section 15 puts an officer in your district. Almost every grievance has one. Nobody says so.

**Turn a hundred grievances into one.** Fifty neighbours, one road is fifty cases, each closed separately — fifty dismissals. Here it is one case, fifty names, one report. More citizens filing means less officer work. One signature per verified mobile, enforced in the database. Two taps to join a public case near you.

**Refuse to close your case.** An officer's report reaches *awaiting your confirmation* and no further. Only the citizen marks it fixed; "not fixed" reopens the same case.

Nothing is filed until the record is complete enough for an office to act on.

Filing into government systems is simulated, and every case says so.
